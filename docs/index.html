What do matrices mean to you?

Why do we need a way to represent an array of rows and columns of numbers, and to execute computations and operations between them?

[2102][−12]=[04]\left[\begin{array}{r} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{r} -1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{l} 0 \\ 4 \end{array}\right][20​12​][−12​]=[04​]

In school, you may recall reluctantly performing drill after drills of matrix-vector multiplications mechanically. You may even have been taught to memorize several inane formulas.

[abcd][xy]=x[ac]+y[bd]=[ax+bycx+dy] \begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{ll} a & b \\ c & d \end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{l} x \\ y \end{array}\right] } & =x\left[\begin{array}{l} a \\ c \end{array}\right]+y\left[\begin{array}{l} b \\ d \end{array}\right] \\ & =\left[\begin{array}{l} a x+b y \\ c x+d y \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} [ac​bd​][xy​]​=x[ac​]+y[bd​]=[ax+bycx+dy​]​

Let's explore a deeper, and a more visual intuition behind what matrices represent.

Multiply a vector by a matrix and you get another vector back — the matrix transforms it. That one idea is what this whole piece is about, but to get there we need to be comfortable with vectors first. So let's start there and build up.

A vector is an arrow, and it only takes two things to pin one down: a direction — the way it points — and a magnitude — how long it is. Change either one and you have a different vector.

Here's one, drawn in blue. Try changing its direction and its length, and watch its coordinates change along with it.

There's a simpler way to change a vector's length: just multiply it by a number. A plain number like this is called a scalar, and multiplying by one is called scaling.

Scaling is easy to predict. A number bigger than 111 stretches the vector, a number between 000 and 111 shrinks it, and a negative number flips it around to point the opposite way. What never changes is the line the vector lies along.

Here's a vector u⃗=i+2j\vec{u}=\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}u=i+2j. Scale it by different numbers and watch what happens.

Try it

Drag the slider to scale this vector — try a negative number to flip it.

Scalar — 1.0

Vectors can also be added together, and there's a neat way to picture it called the head-to-tail rule: slide the second vector so its tail sits on the first vector's tip, then draw a new arrow from the very start to the very end. That new arrow is the sum.

Here are two vectors to try it on — a⃗\vec{a}a in red and b⃗\vec{b}b in green, both starting at the origin.

Now slide b⃗\vec{b}b over so its tail meets a⃗\vec{a}a's tip. Sliding it doesn't change it: same direction, same length.

Draw an arrow from the start of a⃗\vec{a}a to the end of b⃗\vec{b}b and you have the sum, a⃗+b⃗\vec{a}+\vec{b}a+b, in blue. Notice its coordinates are just the two sets of coordinates added up.

Let's bring in a fresh vector, v⃗=−i+2j\vec{v}=-\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}v=−i+2j, and think about what the coordinates of a vector actually represent.

In the xyxyxy-coordinate plane, every vector can be built out of just two building blocks: a vector of length 111 pointing in the xxx-direction, called ı^\hat{\imath}^, and one of length 111 pointing in the yyy-direction, called ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​.

[−12]=−1[10]+2[01]\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \end{array}\right]}=\textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} \end{array}\right] +\textcolor{#bd93f9}{2}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{0} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \end{array}\right] [−12​]=−1[10​]+2[01​] Together, ı^\hat{\imath}^ and ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ are called the standard basis vectors. And now a vector's coordinates mean something concrete: they're just the amounts to scale each basis vector by before adding the two together.

Scaling some vectors and adding the results is called taking a linear combination of them. So every vector in the plane is a linear combination of ı^\hat{\imath}^ and ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​.

[abcd][xy]=x[ac]+y[bd]=[ax+bycx+dy] \begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{ll} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \end{array}\right]} } & =\textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} \end{array}\right]+\textcolor{#bd93f9}{y}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \end{array}\right] \\ & =\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}+\textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}+\textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \end{array}\right]} \end{aligned} [ac​bd​][xy​]​=x[ac​]+y[bd​]=[ax+bycx+dy​]​

Look closely — that's a linear combination too. Multiplying a vector by a matrix scales two vectors by the coordinates and adds them, exactly as before. The only difference is which two vectors: instead of ı^\hat{\imath}^ and ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​, we use the two columns of the matrix.

[2102][−12]=−1[20]+2[12]=[04]\begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \end{array}\right] \textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \end{array}\right]} } & =\textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1}\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} \end{array}\right]+\textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \end{array}\right] \\ & = \left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#04cefb}{0} \\ \textcolor{#04cefb}{4} \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} [20​12​][−12​]​=−1[20​]+2[12​]=[04​]​

So a matrix is really just a way of writing down where you want the basis vectors to go. That's the core insight: a matrix transforms a vector by transforming the basis vectors underneath it, building an entirely new coordinate system.

The transformed vector is still the same linear combination — the same two coordinates doing the scaling — only now it's the new basis vectors being scaled and added.

Because every vector is built from the basis vectors, moving them drags all of space along too. To really feel that, let's stop watching one vector and watch a whole field of them at once, each multiplied by the same matrix.

Arrows everywhere would be a mess, so we'll draw each vector as just a dot at its tip. The grid lines get transformed along with them, with a copy of the original grid left underneath for comparison.

Space rotates and stretches, following wherever the new basis vectors land.

A matrix can't warp space just any way it likes. It performs a particular kind of transformation, called a linear transformation, and you can spot one by two tell-tale signs:

  • Straight lines stay straight — nothing ever curves, and
  • the origin stays exactly where it is.

Watch the grid as it moves and you'll see it: the lines stay parallel and evenly spaced the whole way through.

The Determinant

Every square matrix has a single number attached to it called its determinant. For a 2×22\times22×2 matrix you multiply along each diagonal and subtract one product from the other:

det⁡[abcd]=ad−bc\det\left[\begin{array}{ll} a & b \\ c & d \end{array}\right] = ad - bcdet[ac​bd​]=ad−bc

Let's clear the board and work it out for the matrix we've been using all along. The arithmetic is quick, and it comes to 444:

det⁡[2102]=(2)(2)−(1)(0)=4\begin{aligned} \det\left[\begin{array}{rr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \end{array}\right] & = (\textcolor{#ff57b6}{2})(\textcolor{#aa73f7}{2}) - (\textcolor{#aa73f7}{1})(\textcolor{#ff57b6}{0}) \\ & = 4 \end{aligned}det[20​12​]​=(2)(2)−(1)(0)=4​

So far that's just arithmetic. The interesting question is what the number means — and it turns out to mean something very concrete. The determinant is the factor by which the transformation scales area.

det⁡A=area afterarea before\det A = \frac{\text{area after}}{\text{area before}}detA=area beforearea after​

To see that, let's run the same transformation again, but this time keep an eye on one particular patch of the plane rather than the whole thing.

Here's the patch, after the transformation: the parallelogram built on the two columns of the matrix. Count the grid squares inside it and you get an area of 444.

And here's what that patch was before — the unit square built on ı^\hat{\imath}^ and ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​: one unit wide, one unit tall, area 111.

Divide one by the other and you get the determinant. And this isn't special to the unit square — every region in the plane, whatever its shape, has its area multiplied by 444.

det⁡A=41=4\det A = \frac{\textcolor{#04cefb}{4}}{\textcolor{#fde047}{1}} = 4detA=14​=4

Two special cases are worth seeing for yourself.

First, a negative determinant. This matrix leaves ı^\hat{\imath}^ pointing right but sends ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ downwards, so the grid gets turned over like a sheet of paper:

det⁡[200−1]=(2)(−1)−(0)(0)=−2\det\left[\begin{array}{rr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{0} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{-1} \end{array}\right] = (\textcolor{#ff57b6}{2})(\textcolor{#aa73f7}{-1}) - (\textcolor{#aa73f7}{0})(\textcolor{#ff57b6}{0}) = -2det[20​0−1​]=(2)(−1)−(0)(0)=−2

The area still grows — by a factor of 222 — but the sign tells you something extra: the orientation was reversed. What used to be counter-clockwise from ı^\hat{\imath}^ to ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ is now clockwise. The size of the number is the area factor; the minus sign is the flip.

Second, a determinant of 000. Here both columns point along the same line, so there's nothing to build a parallelogram out of:

det⁡[1224]=(1)(4)−(2)(2)=0\det\left[\begin{array}{rr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{4} \end{array}\right] = (\textcolor{#ff57b6}{1})(\textcolor{#aa73f7}{4}) - (\textcolor{#aa73f7}{2})(\textcolor{#ff57b6}{2}) = 0det[12​24​]=(1)(4)−(2)(2)=0

Watch the whole grid collapse onto a single line. Every bit of area is squashed down to nothing, which is exactly what a determinant of 000 means. The transformation has thrown a dimension away, and once that happens there's no way to undo it — you can't tell which of the squashed points you started from.

Back to our original matrix, then — with a determinant of 444 and space the right way round.

[abcd][xy]=x[ac]+y[bd]=[ax+bycx+dy] \begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{ll} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \end{array}\right]} } & =\textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} \end{array}\right]+\textcolor{#bd93f9}{y}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \end{array}\right] \\ & =\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}+\textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}+\textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \end{array}\right]} \end{aligned} [ac​bd​][xy​]​=x[ac​]+y[bd​]=[ax+bycx+dy​]​

Recap

  • Any vector can be expressed as the addition of scaled basis vectors, i.e. a linear combination of basis vectors.
  • A matrix can be viewed as a way to package information about a linear transformation. The columns of a matrix represent where the new basis vectors land after the transformation.
  • The determinant of a matrix is the factor by which it scales area. A negative determinant means space was also flipped over, and a determinant of 000 means space was squashed flat.
  • Matrix-vector multiplication is a way to compute where a given vector lands after the transformation defined by a matrix.

With our understanding so far, try to tinker about and figure out what kinds of transformations are possible with matrices!

Undoing a Transformation

If a matrix moves space around, a natural question is whether you can move it back. The matrix that does that is called the inverse of AAA, written A−1A^{-1}A−1. Apply one and then the other and you end up exactly where you started — which is the same as saying they multiply to give the matrix that changes nothing.

A−1A=[1001]A^{-1}A = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]A−1A=[10​01​]

For a 2×22\times22×2 matrix there's a recipe: swap the two entries on the main diagonal, flip the sign of the other two, and divide everything by the determinant.

A−1=1det⁡A[d−b−ca]A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\left[\begin{array}{rr} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{array}\right]A−1=detA1​[d−c​−ba​]

Let's watch it happen. Here's the plain grid again, with the unit square marked on it.

Apply AAA and we get the picture from before: the square is sheared into a parallelogram with 444 times the area.

Now apply A−1A^{-1}A−1 on top of that. Space slides back, the parallelogram shrinks to the unit square, and the grid is exactly as it was.

A−1=14[2−102]=[0.5−0.2500.5]A^{-1} = \frac{1}{4}\left[\begin{array}{rr} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.5 & -0.25 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{array}\right]A−1=41​[20​−12​]=[0.50​−0.250.5​]

Look at where the determinant turns up: a factor of 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​ out in front. That makes sense in terms of area. Going forwards, AAA multiplied every area by 444; to undo that, A−1A^{-1}A−1 has to divide every area by 444.

When There's No Way Back

That 1det⁡A\tfrac{1}{\det A}detA1​ should make you suspicious. If the determinant were 000, the recipe would ask you to divide by zero — so it simply can't be carried out. But that's an answer about algebra. The more interesting question is why no such matrix could exist in the first place.

Take two different vectors, p⃗\vec{p}p​ in red and q⃗\vec{q}q​ in green. Right now they're clearly distinct — they point to different places.

Now apply a matrix with a determinant of 000, and watch them. Space flattens onto a line, and the two arrows land on top of one another — the exact same point.

[1224][10]=[12][1224][3−1]=[12]\begin{aligned} \left[\begin{array}{rr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{4} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#f87171}{\left[\begin{array}{r} 1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]} & = \left[\begin{array}{r} 1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] \\ \left[\begin{array}{rr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{4} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#4ade80}{\left[\begin{array}{r} 3 \\ -1 \end{array}\right]} & = \left[\begin{array}{r} 1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned}[12​24​][10​][12​24​][3−1​]​=[12​]=[12​]​

And there's the problem. An inverse would have to take that output and give back the input. But two different vectors went in and the same vector came out, so the question "which one did it come from?" has no single answer. No matrix could do it, because there's nothing for it to return.

Information was destroyed, and no transformation can invent it again. This is what people mean when they call a matrix with det⁡A=0\det A = 0detA=0 singular: it squashes space, and squashing is a one-way trip.

Scroll back up to the playground and drag the matrix until the determinant reads 000. You'll see A−1A^{-1}A−1 disappear the moment it gets there.

Beyond Two-Dimensions

So far we've only been talking about matrix transformations in two-dimensions on the xyxyxy-plane. Do the same intuitions carry over to higher dimensions?

To make up three dimensions, we have yet another standard basis vector — the unit vector in the zzz-direction. This also means we're now fiddling around with vectors of length 333 — representing the xyzxyzxyz coordinates — and matrices of size 3×33\times33×3.

The concept of matrix transformations in 3D is exactly the same. The three basis vectors are transformed to their new locations, warping space along with them. These locations are completely determined by the columns of the matrix.

[11001−1011][−121]−1[100]+2[111]1[0−11]=[113]\begin{aligned} & {\left[\begin{array}{rrr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} & \textcolor{#ffa647}{0} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} & \textcolor{#ffa647}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} & \textcolor{#ffa647}{1} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \\ \textcolor{#ffb86c}{1} \end{array}\right]}} \\ & \textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1}\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} \end{array}\right]+\textcolor{#bd93f9}{2}\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#ffb86c}{1}\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ffa647}{0} \\ \textcolor{#ffa647}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#ffa647}{1} \end{array}\right] \\ & =\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#04cefb}{1} \\ \textcolor{#04cefb}{1} \\ \textcolor{#04cefb}{3} \end{array}\right] \end{aligned}​​100​111​0−11​​​−121​​−1​100​​+2​111​​1​0−11​​=​113​​​

Originally, any vector is composed of a linear combination of these three standard basis vectors. To figure out the where the vector lands after the transformation, it is a linear combination of the transformed basis vectors, each scaled by the respective coordinates in the starting vector.

The Determinant in 3D

The determinant carries over too — with one word changed. In 2D it measured how much a transformation scales area. In 3D there's an extra dimension to stretch, so it measures how much it scales volume.

det⁡A=volume aftervolume before\det A = \frac{\text{volume after}}{\text{volume before}}detA=volume beforevolume after​

Instead of the unit square, we start from the unit cube — the box built on ı^\hat{\imath}^, ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ and k^\hat{k}k^, with a volume of 111.

Apply the transformation and the cube is sheared into a slanted box, built on the three columns of the matrix. For this matrix its volume comes out to 222, so every region of space has its volume doubled:

det⁡[11001−1011]=2\det\left[\begin{array}{rrr} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} & \textcolor{#ffa647}{0} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} & \textcolor{#ffa647}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} & \textcolor{#ffa647}{1} \end{array}\right] = 2det​100​111​0−11​​=2

The two special cases carry over as well. A negative determinant means the transformation turned space inside out, and a determinant of 000 means the cube was flattened — squashed onto a plane, a line, or even a single point.

From these visually-focused examples we've seen thus far, the most obvious application of matrix transformations would be that of computer graphics. In fact, this is precisely how this article was built! Matrices provide a language to rotate, scale and translate vectors and points and consequently entire objects in 2D or 3D space.

Go forth and wrap your head around matrix transformations in 3D! Now you have a whole additional dimension to fidget around with.
